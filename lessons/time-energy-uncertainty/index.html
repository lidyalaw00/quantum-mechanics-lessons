Time-energy uncertainty

How fast can a quantum state change?

Let’s build the intuition from time evolution, then discover the quantum speed limit.

STEP 01

A state with definite energy

We want to understand how fast a quantum state can physically change. First, imagine a system with a time-independent Hamiltonian.

Let us start with a simple example: the system is in a single energy eigenstate |0⟩, with energy E0. As time passes, this state acquires a phase:

|ψ(0)⟩=|0⟩|ψ(t)⟩=e−iE0tℏ|0⟩

Notice that the state is still |0⟩, apart from a phase. The same thing happens for any energy eigenstate |En⟩:

|ψ(0)⟩=|En⟩|ψ(t)⟩=e−iEntℏ|En⟩

The whole state only acquires a global phase. A global phase cannot be detected by any measurement. So the physical state stays the same, even though the state vector changes with time.

Its energy is definite, which means its energy uncertainty is zero: ΔE=0.

STEP 02

What if the energy is uncertain?

Now, instead of just |0⟩, suppose the system is in an equal superposition of two energy eigenstates, |0⟩ and |1⟩, with different energies E₀ and E₁.

|ψ(0)⟩=12(|0⟩+|1⟩)|ψ(t)⟩=12(e−iE0tℏ|0⟩+e−iE1tℏ|1⟩)

Each energy component acquires its own phase. Take out the global phase:

|ψ(t)⟩=e−iE0tℏ12(|0⟩+e−i(E1−E0)tℏ|1⟩)

The first exponential is a global phase and does not matter physically. The second exponential is a relative phase: it changes with time, at a rate set by the energy gap.

Why does this matter? Consider these two states:

|+⟩=12(|0⟩+|1⟩)|−⟩=12(|0⟩−|1⟩)

The two states have the same energy probabilities, but they are orthogonal. A measurement in the plus/minus basis can tell them apart perfectly. A relative phase change can therefore be a real physical change.

This is the intuition: energy uncertainty allows the physical state to evolve, even while the energy itself is conserved.

STEP 03

How fast can an observable change?

How can we measure how fast the physical state changes? Choose an observable A^ and look at its expectation value ⟨A^⟩. If that value changes, we know the physical state has changed.

To describe a meaningful change, we need two things:

How much?

Standard deviation σₐ

A natural scale for a typical variation in A.

How fast?

Rate of change

How quickly the expectation value of A changes.

Like distance divided by speed, we define a characteristic time:

ΔtA=σA|d⟨A^⟩dt|

The equation of motion and Robertson’s uncertainty relation give the following result:

ΔtAΔE≥ℏ2

This is the Mandelstam–Tamm time-energy uncertainty relation. Here ΔE is the standard deviation of the energy (σH).

A small energy uncertainty limits how quickly an observable can change compared with its typical fluctuations. Δtₐ is a characteristic time, not an uncertainty in measuring time.

Show the mathematical derivation

For an observable A that does not explicitly depend on time, the equation of motion is

d⟨A^⟩dt=1iℏ⟨[A^,H^]⟩

Robertson’s uncertainty relation, applied to A and H, is

σAσH≥12|⟨[A^,H^]⟩|

Substitute the equation of motion into the right-hand side:

σAσH≥ℏ2|d⟨A^⟩dt|

If the rate of change is nonzero, divide by its magnitude and use ΔE = σH.

ΔtAΔE≥ℏ2

When the rate is zero, the undivided inequality still holds; the ratio may be undefined.

STEP 04

The quantum speed limit

Now we can ask a more specific question: What is the minimum time required for a quantum state to evolve into a state orthogonal to its initial state?

Why an orthogonal state? Two orthogonal pure states are perfectly distinguishable. So, in other words, we are asking how long it takes for the state to become perfectly distinguishable from its initial state.

We denote this minimum time by t⊥. It is the first time at which

⟨ψ(0)|ψ(t⊥)⟩=0

The squared overlap with the initial state tells us how similar the two states are. It starts at 1 and reaches 0 if the state becomes orthogonal.

For a time-independent Hamiltonian, applying the time-energy uncertainty relation gives the quantum speed limit:

t⊥≥πℏ2ΔE=h4ΔE

There is a minimum time needed to reach an orthogonal state. The larger the energy uncertainty, the smaller this lower bound. Not every state will reach it.

Show how the speed limit is derived

Choose the projector onto the initial state as our observable:

A^=|ψ(0)⟩⟨ψ(0)|

Its expectation value is the survival probability:

P(t)=⟨A^⟩=|⟨ψ(0)|ψ(t)⟩|2

Because A² = A, the standard deviation becomes

σA=P(1−P)

The undivided time-energy inequality gives

|dPdt|≤2ΔEℏP(1−P)

For a time-independent Hamiltonian, ΔE is constant. Integrate the bound along the evolution from P = 1 to P = 0:

t⊥≥ℏ2ΔE∫01dPP(1−P)=πℏ2ΔE

The integral equals π (use P = sin² θ). Since h = 2πℏ, this is the quantum speed limit shown above.

See the simple two-energy example

Return to the equal superposition of |0⟩ and |1⟩ from Step 2. Its energy uncertainty is half the energy gap:

ΔE=|E1−E0|2

It becomes orthogonal when its relative phase has changed by π:

|E1−E0|t⊥ℏ=πt⊥=πℏ|E1−E0|=πℏ2ΔE

This example exactly reaches the Mandelstam–Tamm lower bound.

THE TAKEAWAY

Energy can stay constant while the state changes.

Different energy components acquire different phases. Relative phases can change measurable properties, even when the energy probabilities stay fixed. The Mandelstam–Tamm relation limits how fast this physical change can occur, for a given energy uncertainty.

ΔtAΔE≥ℏ2t⊥≥πℏ2ΔE=h4ΔE
